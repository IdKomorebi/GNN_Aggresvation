\subsection{The Amortized Attacker}\label{sec:res-est}

\paragraph{Fidelity}
Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator} compares the amortized estimates with retrained ground truth for all
sets of size $\le 3$. The estimate of $V$ is biased downward, visibly so for PJM, where the
training set is smallest (bias $-0.03$ to $-0.04$); averaged over the ten targets the error is 0.018 with a signed bias of
$-0.013$, i.e.\ largely common-mode. As predicted by~\eqref{eq:prop}, the field-level
risk is estimated more accurately than the set values it is built from: the mean absolute error of
$\hat M^{(2)}$ is 0.015 and its Spearman correlation with the exact $M^{(2)}$ across fields is
0.968 (Table~\ref{tab:design-main}). Certification recovers most of the exact risk from very few
retrained backgrounds: retraining the single best-scanned background per field yields a lower
bound equal to 96.1\% of the exact $M^{(2)}$ on average, three backgrounds yield 97.8\%, and on
RTS-GMLC and CAISO the top-3 ratio exceeds 99\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator}c). For a field set of
35 candidates this replaces the $1+34+\binom{34}{2}=596$ backgrounds per field of an exhaustive
evaluation by three.

\paragraph{Amplification and dangerous backgrounds}
The profile of Section~\ref{sec:res-amp} is estimated with similar accuracy: the mean absolute error of
$\hat\Gamma^{(2)}$ is 0.014. Identifying the single worst background is harder---the best-scanned background is the worst
one for 55\% of the fields (23--87\% across targets), and the worst one is among the three best-scanned for 74\% and among
the five best-scanned for 79\%---but this matters little, because dangerous backgrounds come in families of nearly tied
members: retraining the three best-scanned backgrounds per field recovers 94.8\% of the total amplification.

\paragraph{Comparison with other estimators}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines} compare the estimator with other ways of obtaining the
inferability of many field sets without retraining the attacker family for each; the implementations and settings are listed in \ref{app:impl}.
\emph{Re-using a model trained on all fields.} Replacing the removed fields by their mean~\cite{williamson2020spvim}
collapses (error of $V$ 0.44): a network trained on all fields cannot predict from three of them. Linearizing it around its
parameters and fitting a ridge correction per set (LazyVI~\cite{gao2022lazyvi}) is accurate (0.037 on 200 sets per data set)
but costs 1.5--9.1\,s per set in the double precision it needs, so that a complete $K=2$ table would take one to eleven hours
per data set; we therefore ran it on these 200 sets only. Warm-starting from the full model and training with early
stopping~\cite{sun2025warmstart} costs 0.18\,s per set and reaches an $M^{(2)}$ error of 0.047. \emph{Reading the prediction
off a network trained under random masks}, the surrogate used to compute Shapley values and other removal-based
explanations~\cite{covert2021explaining,jethani2022fastshap}, is the fastest option (0.2\,ms) but its $M^{(2)}$ error, 0.057, is
almost four times ours: the per-set readout is what makes a masked backbone accurate. \emph{Retraining simple attackers per
set} (linear or quadratic regression) is fast but underfits (0.096--0.144).

The one competitive method is a tabular foundation model, TabPFN v2~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}, used as an in-context
attacker: it was pre-trained once on synthetic data sets and predicts the target of a new data set without training, from the
training rows it receives as context. Before certification it is slightly more accurate than ours for $V$ (0.016 against
0.018) and $M^{(2)}$ (0.013 against 0.015) and less accurate for the amplification $\Gamma^{(2)}$ (0.019 against 0.014); its
advantage is concentrated on PJM, the data set with the fewest training rows, where our estimate is biased downward by
0.03--0.04, whereas on NEM it is clearly worse (error of $V$ 0.024 against 0.010, of $\Gamma^{(2)}$ 0.032 against 0.015). The two recover comparable total marginal risk after certification: retraining the best-scanned background per field recovers 95.9\% of the exact
$M^{(2)}$ with TabPFN and 96.1\% with ours, and three backgrounds recover 97.9\% and 97.8\%. Scan--certify with TabPFN recovers
more critical fields at $\tau=0.7$ (100\% against 79\%, the difference again coming from PJM) and fewer at $\tau=0.5$ (88\%
against 91\%).

The two differ in what they amortize. TabPFN amortizes learning across data sets, so every field set is a new prediction
task: its training rows restricted to $S$ form a new context that passes through an eight-member ensemble of a twelve-layer
transformer with 11 million parameters, once for each target, and nothing computed for one set is reused for the next. Our estimator amortizes across the field sets of one
data set: the representation is learned once, and a query costs forward passes of small networks and three linear solves,
one per seed, each shared by all targets. An audit consists precisely of many such queries---thousands of sets per target and table, repeated at
every re-grading---and here the difference is decisive: TabPFN takes 0.8--7.0\,s per set, 13 to 110 times as long as ours; multiplying these measured costs by the query counts gives about 2.7\,h versus 5\,min for the NEM $K=2$ table, and 12\,h versus 54\,min for the 52,360 four-field CAISO sets.
Background ranking governs how much risk a small verification budget recovers. Profile accuracy also matters for reporting $M^{(0)}$ and $\Gamma^{(K)}$ across fields. The proposed estimator provides both at a per-set cost suited to repeated audits. The small positive bias of TabPFN also shows that it is at times a
stronger attacker than the retrained family (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); such a model is better placed as an additional
member of the attacker family, where it is evaluated only on certified backgrounds, than as the scanner.

\paragraph{Why this estimator configuration}
Table~\ref{tab:design-main} summarizes three design findings. First, nonlinear features are necessary: the raw values and their squares give an $M^{(2)}$ error of 0.096, compared with 0.021 for random features. Reconstruction pre-training reduces it to 0.017 by retaining information beyond the selected targets. Second, adding an untrained feature branch reduces the error further to 0.015; the two branches supply complementary directions to the subset-specific readout. Third, stable feature scaling and prediction bounds prevent rare readout failures from dominating the estimated maximum. Without prediction clipping, the reconstruction model's risk error rises to 0.027 and its top-1 certified ratio falls to 0.881.

The full configuration averages three independently fitted predictions and costs 58\,ms per set on average. We retain it as the fixed estimator because this cost is already practical for the present audit scale and it provides the most accurate overall field profiles among these configurations. Reconstruction alone costs 21\,ms and achieves the same rounded certified ratios (0.961 and 0.978 for one and three backgrounds), with slightly larger risk error. It is a useful alternative when the number of rows or queries makes scanning cost the dominant concern. \ref{app:diagnostics} reports the remaining seed, mask and representation comparisons.
