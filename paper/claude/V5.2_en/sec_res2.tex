\paragraph{Fidelity}
Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator} compares the amortized estimates with retrained ground truth for all
sets of size $\le 3$. The estimate of $V$ is biased downward, visibly so for PJM, where the
training set is smallest (bias $-0.03$ to $-0.04$); averaged over the ten targets the error is 0.018 with a signed bias of
$-0.013$, i.e.\ largely common-mode. As predicted by~\eqref{eq:prop}, the field-level
risk is estimated more accurately than the set values it is built from: the mean absolute error of
$\hat M^{(2)}$ is 0.015 and its Spearman correlation with the exact $M^{(2)}$ across fields is
0.968 (Table~\ref{tab:design}). Certification recovers most of the exact risk from very few
retrained backgrounds: retraining the single best-scanned background per field yields a lower
bound equal to 96.1\% of the exact $M^{(2)}$ on average, three backgrounds yield 97.8\%, and on
RTS-GMLC and CAISO the top-3 ratio exceeds 99\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator}c). For a field set of
35 candidates this replaces the $1+34+\binom{34}{2}=596$ backgrounds per field of an exhaustive
evaluation by three.

\paragraph{Amplification and dangerous backgrounds}
The profile of Section~\ref{sec:res-amp} is estimated with similar accuracy: the mean absolute error of
$\hat\Gamma^{(2)}$ is 0.014. Identifying the single worst background is harder---the best-scanned background is the worst
one for 55\% of the fields (23--87\% across targets), and the worst one is among the three best-scanned for 74\% and among
the five best-scanned for 79\%---but this matters little, because dangerous backgrounds come in families of nearly tied
members: retraining the three best-scanned backgrounds per field recovers 94.8\% of the total amplification.

\paragraph{Design choices}
Table~\ref{tab:design} justifies the configuration of the estimator; every row uses the same per-set readout.
\emph{Feature map.} Without a backbone, a readout on the raw values and squares of the fields in $S$ is far less accurate
($M^{(2)}$ error 0.096); any nonlinear feature map helps, and the maps order as untrained network (0.021), target-only
pre-training (0.021), reconstruction pre-training (0.017), and reconstruction concatenated with an untrained network (0.015).
\emph{Seeds.} Averaging the predictions of three seeds, each with its own readout, lowers the error of $V$ from 0.020 to
0.018 and removes the variation between seeds (with one seed, critical-field recall at $\tau=0.5$ varies between 91\% and
98\%). Concatenating the features of three seeds into one readout is not an alternative: for target-only backbones it is
slower (41 against 21\,ms per set, because the cost of a readout grows with the feature dimension) and certifies worse
(top-1 ratio 0.916 against 0.953 on the six RTS-GMLC and NEM targets where it was evaluated). \emph{Safeguards.} Without
the prediction clip, a reconstruction backbone overestimates $M^{(2)}$ (error 0.027, certified top-1 ratio 0.881); without
any safeguard a target-only backbone fares similarly (0.036, 0.825). \emph{Masks.} A backbone pre-trained on fully visible
inputs is poor (0.036); how visible fields are sampled matters little (Bernoulli versus uniform visible count: 0.024 versus
0.023); and the raw features $[x_S, x_S^2]$ help slightly (0.025 without them). Why the pre-training objective matters so
little on its own, and why the combination with an untrained network works best, is the subject of the next two paragraphs.

\paragraph{Why target-only masked pre-training adds little}
One result of the ablation is counter-intuitive: an untrained, randomly initialized backbone with the same per-set
readout estimates $V$ and $M^{(2)}$ about as well as the backbones pre-trained on the targets
(Table~\ref{tab:design}). Three findings explain it (Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}).
\emph{(i) Collapse.} A backbone trained only to predict one to three targets compresses its hidden layer onto the few
directions that predict them on average over masks: the effective dimension of the representation (participation
ratio) is 1.60, against 3.30 for an untrained network. Restricting pre-training masks to one to four visible
fields, the size of audit queries, does not change this (1.65), so the cause is the narrow objective, not the mask
distribution. \emph{(ii) What the readout uses.} Truncating the features of each set to their $r$ leading principal
components shows that pre-training does find target-aligned directions---at $r=2$ its error is half that of untrained
features---but that the readout draws most of its accuracy from the remaining directions, where random ReLU features
serve as well (Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}a). A ridge readout on 256 random features is a random-feature approximation of a
kernel regression~\cite{rahimi2007random}, which is already adequate for a function of at most three fields learned
from several thousand rows. \emph{(iii) Set size.} The gap opens for larger sets: for sets of 16 fields the error of
untrained features is 0.074, against 0.027 for target-only and 0.023 for reconstruction pre-training
(Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}b; gradient-boosting ground truth). Audit queries are small, which is where pre-training matters
least.

\paragraph{From the diagnosis to the estimator}
Adding the reconstruction term of~\eqref{eq:pretrain} restores the effective dimension (2.75) and lowers the error of
$V$ by 24\% (three seeds, sets of size $\le3$ and of size four) and that of the marginals by 18\%. At first, $M^{(2)}$ did
not improve (0.027 against 0.021). Because $\Mik$ is a maximum over marginals, its error is governed by the upper tail of
the marginal errors, not by their mean (Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}c), and the tail had a single cause: reconstructing the
heavy-tailed price and reserve columns produces units that are almost constant on the training rows except for spikes.
For the single-field sets of PJM reserve totals a few test predictions explode and $\hat V=0$ where the truth is about
0.3, and every field evaluated over that background is overestimated. Clipping predictions to the range of the target
on the training rows removes this failure without using any test information. Finding (ii) suggests the last step:
concatenating the reconstruction features with those of an untrained network, so that the readout receives both the
target-aligned directions and the random bulk. Table~\ref{tab:design} lists the candidates; we fixed the rule for choosing
among them---the configuration that is not worse than the others on the largest number of ten accuracy and decision
metrics, and not worse than target-only pre-training on any---before evaluating the last candidate. The concatenation with
three seeds satisfies it on nine of ten metrics and is our estimator: relative to target-only pre-training, the error of
$V$ falls by 32\% (sets of size $\le3$) and 32\% (size four), that of $M^{(2)}$ by 29\% and that of $\Gamma^{(2)}$ by 19\%;
the certified top-1 ratio rises from 0.934 to 0.961, the worst background is among the three best-scanned ones for 74\%
instead of 67\% of the fields, and scan--certify without a margin recovers 91\% instead of 88\% of the critical fields at
$\tau=0.5$ (79\% instead of 75\% at $\tau=0.7$). The one metric on which it is not the best is this recall at $\tau=0.5$,
where the one-seed concatenation reaches 93\% on average (91--98\% across seeds). Decisions move less than estimates:
with the conservative margin $\delta=0.05$ all configurations recover 75--80\% of the critical fields, and even the
untrained backbone recovers 92\% at $\tau=0.5$ without a margin. With clipping in place, target-only pre-training no longer
beats the untrained backbone on any metric: its earlier advantage in certification came from being less prone to readout
explosions, not from better information.

\paragraph{Comparison with other estimators}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines} compare the estimator with other ways of obtaining the
inferability of many field sets without retraining the attacker family for each; all are run with their default settings.
\emph{Re-using a model trained on all fields.} Replacing the removed fields by their mean~\cite{williamson2020spvim}
collapses (error of $V$ 0.44): a network trained on all fields cannot predict from three of them. Linearizing it around its
parameters and fitting a ridge correction per set (LazyVI~\cite{gao2022lazyvi}) is accurate (0.037 on 200 sets per data set)
but costs 1.5--9.1\,s per set in the double precision it needs, so that a complete $K=2$ table would take one to eleven hours
per data set; we therefore ran it on these 200 sets only. Warm-starting from the full model and training with early
stopping~\cite{sun2025warmstart} costs 0.18\,s per set and reaches an $M^{(2)}$ error of 0.047. \emph{Reading the prediction
off a network trained under random masks}, the surrogate used to compute Shapley values and other removal-based
explanations~\cite{covert2021explaining,jethani2022fastshap}, is the fastest option (0.2\,ms) but its $M^{(2)}$ error, 0.057, is
almost four times ours: the per-set readout is what makes a masked backbone accurate. \emph{Retraining simple attackers per
set} (linear or quadratic regression) is fast but underfits (0.096--0.144).

The one competitive method is a tabular foundation model used as an in-context attacker, TabPFN
v2~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}, which was pre-trained on synthetic data and predicts the target from the training rows of $S$
without any training. It is slightly more accurate than ours for $V$ (0.016 against 0.018) and $M^{(2)}$ (0.013 against
0.015), less accurate for the amplification $\Gamma^{(2)}$ (0.019 against 0.014), equal in certification (top-1 ratio 0.959
against 0.961), and recovers more critical fields at $\tau=0.7$ (100\% against 79\%) but fewer at $\tau=0.5$ (88\% against
91\%). Its advantage is concentrated on PJM, the data set with the fewest rows, where our estimate is biased downward by
0.03--0.04 and misses critical fields at the higher threshold; on NEM it is clearly worse (error of $V$ 0.024 against 0.010,
of $\Gamma^{(2)}$ 0.032 against 0.015). Its small positive bias shows that it is at times a stronger attacker than the retrained
family, which is limitation (i) of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} in action. It costs 0.8--7.0\,s per set, 13 to 110 times ours:
a $K=2$ table for NEM takes 2.7\,h instead of 5\,min, and the 52,360 sets of size four for CAISO take 12\,h instead of 54\,min.
Because every decision is certified by retraining, the scan has to rank backgrounds, not to be exact, and it has to be
repeated; our estimator does this at a fraction of the cost with comparable accuracy, and a foundation model is better
placed as an additional member of the attacker family than as the scanner.

\paragraph{One pre-training for all target definitions}
In practice the auditor's target list and candidate pool change---a field is reclassified, a new
target is added. With masked reconstruction over all 53 columns of a market, one backbone serves
every such definition through the readout alone. Table~\ref{tab:backbone} compares it with
backbones supervised on each group's own targets. The all-column backbone is more accurate for
$V$ (0.040 versus 0.049) and slightly more accurate for $M^{(2)}$ (0.026 versus 0.029), and its
certified top-1 ratio rises from 0.906 to 0.944.

The mechanism is visible in the representation. A backbone that serves one or two targets leaves a
quarter of its hidden units inactive (24\%) and has an effective dimension of 1.45; one that must
reconstruct every column keeps 92\% of units active and has an effective dimension of 2.44. It has
to encode the latent operating state---load level, renewable output, congestion---that drives all
grid quantities, which is exactly the argument for multi-task representation
learning~\cite{caruana1997multitask,baxter2000inductive,maurer2016benefit} (Fig.~\ref{fig:scope}). Why $V$ improves more
than $M$ follows from~\eqref{eq:prop}: 89--98\% of the $V$ error is common-mode, and a better
representation mostly shrinks this common part, which cancels in the marginals anyway.

The last row of Table~\ref{tab:backbone} tests a target never seen in pre-training: the target
column is deleted from the data before pre-training. Set values and marginals are estimated as well
as with the all-column backbone, but $\hat M^{(2)}$ is inflated (bias $+0.028$), because the
maximum over many marginals picks up the few that are overestimated; the certified top-1 ratio drops
to 0.75. Retraining three backgrounds per field restores it to 0.966. Re-use for unseen targets
therefore requires certification---which is the pipeline we use anyway.
