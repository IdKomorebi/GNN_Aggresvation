\subsection{Controlled Power-System Mechanism}\label{sec:res-mech}

Before turning to real markets we test the definitions on a case whose inference mechanism is known
in advance. We take the RTS-GMLC market of Section~\ref{sec:data} and give one generating unit,
313\_CC\_1 (a 355\,MW combined-cycle unit that is partly loaded in about 90\% of hours and often sets
the price), a private hourly offer markup: all its offer segments are multiplied by $(1+\kappa_t)$,
with $\kappa_t$ drawn independently each hour. Offers are private information under the disclosure
rules, and $\kappa_t$ is the sensitive target. We clear all 8,784 hours again and assemble eleven
fields: the unit's output $P_g$, the LMP at its bus, the LMP at a nearby bus of the same area, the
LMP at a bus behind the most frequently congested line (C6), an LMP in another area, system and area
load, available wind and solar, the congestion indicator of C6, and the output of a competing unit.
With eleven fields every one of the 2,047 subsets is retrained, so $\Mik$ is exact for every $K$ up to
the full MCI.

\paragraph{Prediction}
When the unit is marginal, its bus price equals $(1+\kappa_t)\,c_s$, where $c_s$ is the cost of the
segment in which it operates, and $P_g$ identifies that segment. Hence $\{P_g,\lambda_g\}$ reveals
$\kappa_t$ while neither field does so alone; a nearby price should substitute for $\lambda_g$ when the
network is uncongested, a price behind a congested line should not, and loads, renewables and the
congestion flag can only indicate whether the unit is marginal. We use small markups,
$\kappa_t\sim U(-5\%,5\%)$, which leave the merit order almost unchanged; larger markups
($\pm30\%$) change the dispatch itself and are reported as a contrast.

\paragraph{Outcome}
Table~\ref{tab:mech} and Fig.~\ref{fig:mech} confirm each prediction. Every price field has zero
single-field risk and a budgeted risk of $0.10$--$0.15$: its risk is \emph{entirely} background
amplification, and the witness is always the unit's output. The gain of $P_g$ itself is $0.065$ alone
and ranks its backgrounds exactly as the mechanism implies---own-bus price ($0.215$), nearby price
($0.191$), other-area price ($0.172$), remote price ($0.163$), competitor output ($0.151$), and
loads, renewables and congestion ($\approx0.07$). The risk also depends on the operating regime: in
hours when C6 is congested the remote price loses its value as a background (gradient-boosting attacker; the joint inferability
with $P_g$ falls from $0.185$ to $0.012$), whereas the own-bus price becomes more informative ($0.198$ to
$0.289$). Because the mechanism is pairwise, $M^{(1)}=M^{(2)}=\dots=\mathrm{MCI}$ for every price field.
With $\pm30\%$ markups the output alone already reveals much of the markup ($M^{(0)}=0.52$), and the
prices remain amplified from zero to about $0.14$: the smaller the markup, the more completely it
hides from single-field screening, but not from the combination.

The scores of Section~\ref{sec:setup} behave as Table~\ref{tab:req} predicts (Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}c).
Correlation, single-field retraining and pairwise inference-graph propagation assign about zero to
every price. SAGE assigns $0.003$ to the own-bus price, because its credit is shared among four
mutually substitutable prices; LOCO likewise sees almost nothing once another price is present, and
permutation importance recognizes only the one price the fitted model happens to use. $M^{(2)}$ flags
all four prices and the competitor output and names the unit output as their background.

\subsection{Background Amplification in Real Data}\label{sec:res-amp}

\paragraph{Single-field grading misses most of the risk}
\input{tables/tab_risk}

We first ask how much of the risk in the four data sets is invisible to single-field grading.
Table~\ref{tab:risk} and Fig.~\ref{fig:risk} report, for each of the ten targets, how many
candidate fields are unsafe on their own and how many become critical only in combination.

Of the 264 field--target
pairs, 256 are critical at $\tau=0.5$ with at most two background fields, and 223 of these are
safe on their own; at $\tau=0.7$ the figures are 138 and 113. For four targets (NEM BW01 and
TUMUT3, PJM load and PJM net interchange) no candidate exceeds $\tau=0.5$ alone, yet 22 to 30
candidates take part in a dangerous combination; at $\tau=0.7$ the same holds for RTS line C35,
where 21 of 23 fields are critical and none is unsafe alone. Withholding every field that is unsafe alone
breaks almost none of the minimal unsafe sets (column ``left''): 1,766 of 1,799 at $\tau=0.5$
and 534 of 559 at $\tau=0.7$ remain.

\emph{Where single fields do leak, combinations still dominate.} Some targets are exposed by a
single close proxy: RTS unit outputs are almost determined by a nodal price ($V_1=0.99$), and
PJM total generation by the system load forecast ($0.97$). For these, $M^{(0)}$ already flags
the proxy, and the remaining risk is again combinatorial (e.g.\ 22 of 24 PJM fields are
combination-critical). Removing such near-proxies from the candidate pool
(Section~\ref{sec:data}) lowers the best single field to $0.42$ for both PJM total generation
and CAISO load but leaves every remaining field combination-critical at $\tau=0.5$ (22/22 and
26/26), so the conclusion does not depend on where the proxy boundary is drawn.

\emph{The witnesses are physically meaningful.} Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} shows the field profile
of RTS line C35 and the gain matrix of NEM unit PPCCGT. The largest increases come from
combinations that reconstruct a residual quantity; for instance, the price at RTS bus 303 rises
from $0.36$ to $0.65$ for the C35 flow together with a zonal load forecast and the congestion
indicator of the neighbouring line C6 (further examples follow below). The gain matrix also shows that the same field is
harmless next to some partners and decisive next to others, which a grade that ignores the background
cannot represent.

\paragraph{Contextual gains are widely spread}
Fig.~\ref{fig:amp}(b) plots, for the most amplified field of six targets, the gain $\Delta_i(T)$ over
every background of at most two fields. The spread is large, and the maximum typically lies far
from the bulk: the South Australian wind forecast has gain $0.19$ alone, $0.20$ on average over
backgrounds, and $0.60$ over its worst background (regional available capacity and solar forecast,
which together with wind give the residual demand the gas unit PPCCGT covers). Over all 264
field--target pairs, the median ratio of the average contextual gain to the maximum is $0.32$: an
average-based score would report about a third of the worst-case risk.

\paragraph{Amplification is common}
Averaged over the 264 pairs, $\Gamma^{(1)}=0.082$, $\Gamma^{(2)}=0.110$ and $\Gamma^{(3)}=0.119$; one
background field supplies 75\% of the total amplification at $K=2$, and $K=2$ supplies 93\% of that
at $K=3$. In 91 pairs the single-field risk is below $0.3$ while the amplification exceeds $0.1$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:amp}a)---fields that look unremarkable alone and become informative in combination.
The largest amplifications are the SA wind forecast for PPCCGT ($0.19\to0.60$), the SP15
day-ahead energy price for CAISO load ($0.14\to0.51$, with the NP15 wind and SP15 solar forecasts:
price and renewables determine net load), and the SA available capacity for PPCCGT ($0.02\to0.36$).
On every pair, $\Gamma^{(1)}$ equals the strongest positive pairwise interaction
(Proposition~\ref{prop:k1}) to numerical precision.

\paragraph{Dangerous backgrounds come in families}
The top backgrounds of a field are often nearly tied and share a core partner. For the SA wind
forecast the five best backgrounds reach gains between $0.545$ and $0.603$, and all contain the SA
available capacity. We therefore report the top-$r$ backgrounds rather than a single witness; the
shared partner is the actionable information (``do not co-release with'').

\subsection{Comparison of Field-Level Risk Scores}\label{sec:res-def}

The mechanism case (Section~\ref{sec:res-mech}) already shows how each family of scores fails on a
known structure; a purely synthetic example with the same three failure modes---synergy, redundancy
and indirect contribution---is given in Appendix~\ref{app:toy}. Here we compare the scores as rankings of the
fields of the ten real targets, against critical fields defined by retrained ground truth.

\paragraph{Ranking}
Table~\ref{tab:ranking} compares the scores as rankings. At $\tau=0.7$, $\hat M^{(2)}$, its certified
version and the exact $M^{(2)}$ share the highest R-precision (0.894, against 0.866 for mutual information, 0.830 for the
inference graph, 0.803 for single-field retraining, 0.769 for SAGE and 0.727 for LOCO), and withholding fields in
the order of certified $M^{(2)}$ exceeds the optimum by only 0.3 fields (exact $M^{(2)}$: 0.4). At $\tau=0.5$
almost every field is critical and every score reaches an R-precision of about 0.98; here
single-field retraining ranks as well as $M^{(2)}$. As a \emph{ranking}, single-field risk is thus a strong
baseline when most fields are dangerous; the advantage of $\Mik$ lies in the decisions it supports and at high
thresholds. Attribution scores computed on one fitted
model (LOCO, permutation importance, SAGE) are the weakest throughout, because they measure what
that model uses rather than what an adversary can infer.

\subsection{The Amortized Attacker}\label{sec:res-est}

\paragraph{Fidelity}
Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator} compares the amortized estimates with retrained ground truth for all
sets of size $\le 3$. The estimate of $V$ is biased downward, visibly so for PJM, where the
training set is smallest (bias $-0.03$ to $-0.04$); averaged over the ten targets the error is 0.018 with a signed bias of
$-0.013$, i.e.\ largely common-mode. As predicted by~\eqref{eq:prop}, the field-level
risk is estimated more accurately than the set values it is built from: the mean absolute error of
$\hat M^{(2)}$ is 0.015 and its Spearman correlation with the exact $M^{(2)}$ across fields is
0.968 (Table~\ref{tab:design}). Certification recovers most of the exact risk from very few
retrained backgrounds: retraining the single best-scanned background per field yields a lower
bound equal to 96.1\% of the exact $M^{(2)}$ on average, three backgrounds yield 97.8\%, and on
RTS-GMLC and CAISO the top-3 ratio exceeds 99\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:estimator}c). For a field set of
35 candidates this replaces the $1+34+\binom{34}{2}=596$ backgrounds per field of an exhaustive
evaluation by three.

\paragraph{Amplification and dangerous backgrounds}
The profile of Section~\ref{sec:res-amp} is estimated with similar accuracy: the mean absolute error of
$\hat\Gamma^{(2)}$ is 0.014. Identifying the single worst background is harder---the best-scanned background is the worst
one for 55\% of the fields (23--87\% across targets), and the worst one is among the three best-scanned for 74\% and among
the five best-scanned for 79\%---but this matters little, because dangerous backgrounds come in families of nearly tied
members: retraining the three best-scanned backgrounds per field recovers 94.8\% of the total amplification.

\paragraph{Design choices}
Table~\ref{tab:design} justifies the configuration of the estimator; every row uses the same per-set readout.
\emph{Feature map.} Without a backbone, a readout on the raw values and squares of the fields in $S$ is far less accurate
($M^{(2)}$ error 0.096); any nonlinear feature map helps, and the maps order as untrained network (0.021), target-only
pre-training (0.021), reconstruction pre-training (0.017), and reconstruction concatenated with an untrained network (0.015).
\emph{Seeds.} Averaging the predictions of three seeds, each with its own readout, lowers the error of $V$ from 0.020 to
0.018 and removes the variation between seeds (with one seed, critical-field recall at $\tau=0.5$ varies between 91\% and
98\%). Concatenating the features of three seeds into one readout is not an alternative: for target-only backbones it is
slower (41 against 21\,ms per set, because the cost of a readout grows with the feature dimension) and certifies worse
(top-1 ratio 0.916 against 0.953 on the six RTS-GMLC and NEM targets where it was evaluated). \emph{Safeguards.} Without
the prediction clip, a reconstruction backbone overestimates $M^{(2)}$ (error 0.027, certified top-1 ratio 0.881); without
any safeguard a target-only backbone fares similarly (0.036, 0.825). \emph{Masks.} A backbone pre-trained on fully visible
inputs is poor (0.036); how visible fields are sampled matters little (Bernoulli versus uniform visible count: 0.024 versus
0.023); and the raw features $[x_S, x_S^2]$ help slightly (0.025 without them). Why the pre-training objective matters so
little on its own, and why the combination with an untrained network works best, is the subject of the next two paragraphs.

\paragraph{Why target-only masked pre-training adds little}
One result of the design comparison is unexpected: an untrained, randomly initialized backbone with the same per-set
readout estimates $V$ and $M^{(2)}$ about as well as the backbones pre-trained on the targets
(Table~\ref{tab:design}). Three findings explain it (Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}).
\emph{(i) Collapse.} A backbone trained only to predict one to three targets compresses its hidden layer onto the few
directions that predict them on average over masks: the effective dimension of the representation (participation
ratio) is 1.60, against 3.30 for an untrained network. Restricting pre-training masks to one to four visible
fields, the size of audit queries, does not change this (1.65), so the cause is the narrow objective, not the mask
distribution. \emph{(ii) What the readout uses.} Truncating the features of each set to their $r$ leading principal
components shows that pre-training does find target-aligned directions---at $r=2$ its error is half that of untrained
features---but that the readout draws most of its accuracy from the remaining directions, where random ReLU features
serve as well (Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}a). A ridge readout on 256 random features is a random-feature approximation of a
kernel regression~\cite{rahimi2007random}, which is already adequate for a function of at most three fields learned
from several thousand rows. \emph{(iii) Set size.} The gap opens for larger sets: for sets of 16 fields the error of
untrained features is 0.074, against 0.027 for target-only and 0.023 for reconstruction pre-training
(Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}b; gradient-boosting ground truth). Audit queries are small, which is where pre-training matters
least.

\paragraph{From the diagnosis to the estimator}
Adding the reconstruction term of~\eqref{eq:pretrain} restores the effective dimension (2.75) and lowers the error of
$V$ by 24\% (three seeds, sets of size $\le3$ and of size four) and that of the marginals by 18\%. At first, $M^{(2)}$ did
not improve (0.027 against 0.021). Because $\Mik$ is a maximum over marginals, its error is governed by the upper tail of
the marginal errors, not by their mean (Fig.~\ref{fig:masking}c), and the tail had a single cause: reconstructing the
heavy-tailed price and reserve columns produces units that are almost constant on the training rows except for spikes.
For the single-field sets of PJM reserve totals a few test predictions explode and $\hat V=0$ where the truth is about
0.3, and every field evaluated over that background is overestimated. Clipping predictions to the range of the target
on the training rows removes this failure without using any test information. Finding (ii) suggests the last step:
concatenating the reconstruction features with those of an untrained network, so that the readout receives both the
target-aligned directions and the random bulk. Table~\ref{tab:design} lists the candidates; we fixed the rule for choosing
among them---the configuration that is not worse than the others on the largest number of ten accuracy and decision
metrics, and not worse than target-only pre-training on any---before evaluating the last candidate. The concatenation with
three seeds satisfies it on nine of ten metrics and is our estimator: relative to target-only pre-training, the error of
$V$ falls by 32\% on sets of size $\le3$ and of size four, that of $M^{(2)}$ by 29\% and that of $\Gamma^{(2)}$ by 19\%;
the certified top-1 ratio rises from 0.934 to 0.961, the worst background is among the three best-scanned ones for 74\%
instead of 67\% of the fields, and scan--certify without a margin recovers 91\% instead of 88\% of the critical fields at
$\tau=0.5$ (79\% instead of 75\% at $\tau=0.7$). The one metric on which it is not the best is this recall at $\tau=0.5$,
where the one-seed concatenation reaches 93\% on average (91--98\% across seeds). Decisions move less than estimates:
with the conservative margin $\delta=0.05$ all configurations recover 75--80\% of the critical fields, and even the
untrained backbone recovers 92\% at $\tau=0.5$ without a margin. With clipping in place, target-only pre-training no longer
beats the untrained backbone on any metric: its earlier advantage in certification came from being less prone to readout
explosions, not from better information.

\paragraph{Comparison with other estimators}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baselines} compare the estimator with other ways of obtaining the
inferability of many field sets without retraining the attacker family for each; all are run with their default settings.
\emph{Re-using a model trained on all fields.} Replacing the removed fields by their mean~\cite{williamson2020spvim}
collapses (error of $V$ 0.44): a network trained on all fields cannot predict from three of them. Linearizing it around its
parameters and fitting a ridge correction per set (LazyVI~\cite{gao2022lazyvi}) is accurate (0.037 on 200 sets per data set)
but costs 1.5--9.1\,s per set in the double precision it needs, so that a complete $K=2$ table would take one to eleven hours
per data set; we therefore ran it on these 200 sets only. Warm-starting from the full model and training with early
stopping~\cite{sun2025warmstart} costs 0.18\,s per set and reaches an $M^{(2)}$ error of 0.047. \emph{Reading the prediction
off a network trained under random masks}, the surrogate used to compute Shapley values and other removal-based
explanations~\cite{covert2021explaining,jethani2022fastshap}, is the fastest option (0.2\,ms) but its $M^{(2)}$ error, 0.057, is
almost four times ours: the per-set readout is what makes a masked backbone accurate. \emph{Retraining simple attackers per
set} (linear or quadratic regression) is fast but underfits (0.096--0.144).

The one competitive method is a tabular foundation model, TabPFN v2~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}, used as an in-context
attacker: it was pre-trained once on synthetic data sets and predicts the target of a new data set without training, from the
training rows it receives as context. Before certification it is slightly more accurate than ours for $V$ (0.016 against
0.018) and $M^{(2)}$ (0.013 against 0.015) and less accurate for the amplification $\Gamma^{(2)}$ (0.019 against 0.014); its
advantage is concentrated on PJM, the data set with the fewest training rows, where our estimate is biased downward by
0.03--0.04, whereas on NEM it is clearly worse (error of $V$ 0.024 against 0.010, of $\Gamma^{(2)}$ 0.032 against 0.015). After
certification the two are indistinguishable: retraining the best-scanned background per field recovers 95.9\% of the exact
$M^{(2)}$ with TabPFN and 96.1\% with ours, and three backgrounds recover 97.9\% and 97.8\%. Scan--certify with TabPFN recovers
more critical fields at $\tau=0.7$ (100\% against 79\%, the difference again coming from PJM) and fewer at $\tau=0.5$ (88\%
against 91\%).

The two differ in what they amortize. TabPFN amortizes learning across data sets, so every field set is a new prediction
task: its training rows restricted to $S$ form a new context that passes through an eight-member ensemble of a twelve-layer
transformer with 11 million parameters, once for each target, and nothing computed for one set is reused for the next. Our estimator amortizes across the field sets of one
data set: the representation is learned once, and a query costs forward passes of small networks and three linear solves,
one per seed, each shared by all targets. An audit consists precisely of many such queries---thousands of sets per target and table, repeated at
every re-grading---and here the difference is decisive: TabPFN takes 0.8--7.0\,s per set, 13 to 110 times as long as ours; a
$K=2$ table for NEM takes 2.7\,h instead of 5\,min, and the 52,360 sets of size four for CAISO take 12\,h instead of 54\,min.
Since flags are confirmed by retraining, the scan has to rank backgrounds rather than predict exact values, and our estimator
provides this ranking at a small fraction of the cost. The small positive bias of TabPFN also shows that it is at times a
stronger attacker than the retrained family (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); such a model is better placed as an additional
member of the attacker family, where it is evaluated only on certified backgrounds, than as the scanner.

\subsection{Robustness and the Choice of $K$}\label{sec:res-robust}

\paragraph{Attacker seeds and data splits}
Ground truth is itself an estimate: attackers are trained with random initializations and
early-stopping splits, and $\Vy$ is measured on one random test split. We repeated the complete
three-attacker retraining with two further seeds (RTS-GMLC, three targets; PJM load) and on two
further random 70/30 splits (RTS-GMLC). Across these reruns the mean absolute change in $\Vy$ is
0.001--0.008, the Spearman correlation of $M^{(2)}$ across fields with the main run is 0.983--0.999,
the set of $\tau$-critical fields is identical at $\tau=0.5$ (Jaccard 1.0 in every case) and agrees
with Jaccard 0.93--1.00 at $\tau=0.7$, and the number of combination-critical fields changes by at
most one or two fields (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}c). The conclusions of
Sections~\ref{sec:res-amp}--\ref{sec:res-def} are therefore not artefacts of a particular seed or
split.

\paragraph{Threshold}
Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}(d) sweeps $\tau$ from 0.3 to 0.9 over all ten targets. At every threshold the
share of fields that are safe alone but critical with at most two background fields exceeds the share
of fields that are unsafe alone (e.g.\ 43\% versus 10\% at $\tau=0.7$). The gap between the two grading
philosophies is thus not tied to the choice of $\tau$, although its size is
(Section~\ref{sec:application}).

\paragraph{Choice of $K$}
Exhaustive retraining of all sets of four fields on every data set (for NEM an additional 27,405 sets)
gives the exact $M^{(3)}$. Measured by the \emph{marginal} risk, $K=2$ is close to saturation:
$M^{(2)}/M^{(3)}$, the ratio of mean field risk, lies between 0.958 and 1.000 for nine targets and is
0.813 for NEM TUMUT3, a hydro unit whose dispatch depends on inter-regional exchange
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}a). The first background field brings most of the amplification ($M^{(1)}/M^{(2)}$
between 0.73 and 0.97).

Threshold crossings, however, do not saturate as quickly, which is the caveat stated in
Section~\ref{sec:mk}: a small increase of $\Mik$ can move a field across $\tau$. At $\tau=0.7$ several
targets have fields that become critical only with a third background field: PJM net interchange (3
critical fields at $K=2$, 21 at $K=3$), NEM PPCCGT (10 and 24) and PJM load (14 and 22)
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}b). At $\tau=0.5$ all fields are already critical at $K=2$ except for TUMUT3 (22
versus 30). We read the two findings together: $K=2$ is sufficient to describe and rank the risk of a
field---the amplification profile of Section~\ref{sec:res-amp} is essentially complete at $K=2$---while
any statement tied to a threshold is a statement about a specific budget and should be reported with
it; an auditor who assumes a stronger adversary evaluates $K=3$. Evaluating all sets of size four is
the workload for which the amortized scan is needed: for CAISO it comprises 52,360 sets per
target, against 7,175 for $K=2$.
