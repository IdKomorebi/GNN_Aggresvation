\section{Results I: Combination Risk that Single-Field Grading Misses}\label{sec:res-risk}
%===============================================================================

\input{tables/tab_risk}

Table~\ref{tab:risk} and Fig.~\ref{fig:risk} report, for each of the ten targets, how many
candidate fields are unsafe on their own and how many become critical only in combination.
Three patterns stand out.

\emph{Most critical fields are invisible to single-field grading.} Of the 264 field--target
pairs, 256 are critical at $\tau=0.5$ with at most two background fields, and 223 of these are
safe on their own; at $\tau=0.7$ the figures are 138 and 113. For four targets (NEM BW01 and
TUMUT3, PJM load and PJM net interchange) no candidate exceeds $\tau=0.5$ alone, yet 22 to 30
candidates take part in a dangerous combination; at $\tau=0.7$ the same holds for RTS line C35,
where 21 of 23 fields are critical and none is unsafe alone. Withholding every field that is unsafe alone
breaks almost none of the minimal unsafe sets (column ``left''): 1,766 of 1,799 at $\tau=0.5$
and 534 of 559 at $\tau=0.7$ remain.

\emph{Where single fields do leak, combinations still dominate.} Some targets are exposed by a
single close proxy: RTS unit outputs are almost determined by a nodal price ($V_1=0.99$), and
PJM total generation by the system load forecast ($0.97$). For these, $M^{(0)}$ already flags
the proxy, and the remaining risk is again combinatorial (e.g.\ 22 of 24 PJM fields are
combination-critical). Removing such near-proxies from the candidate pool
(Section~\ref{sec:data}) lowers the best single field to $0.42$ for both PJM total generation
and CAISO load but leaves every remaining field combination-critical at $\tau=0.5$ (22/22 and
26/26), so the conclusion does not depend on where the proxy boundary is drawn.

\emph{The witnesses are physically meaningful.} Fig.~\ref{fig:profile} shows the field profile
of RTS line C35 and the gain matrix of NEM unit PPCCGT. The largest escalations come from
combinations that reconstruct a residual quantity: the SP15 day-ahead energy price rises from
$M^{(0)}=0.14$ to $M^{(2)}=0.51$ for CAISO load with the NP15 wind and SP15 solar forecasts as
witnesses (price and renewables determine net load); the South Australian wind forecast rises
from $0.19$ to $0.60$ for PPCCGT together with the regional available capacity and solar
forecast (the residual demand the gas unit is dispatched for); the price at RTS bus 303 rises
from $0.36$ to $0.65$ for the C35 flow together with a zonal load forecast and the congestion
indicator of the neighbouring line C6. The gain matrix also shows that the same field is
harmless next to some partners and decisive next to others, which is what a grade that ignores
the background cannot represent.

\section{Results II: Comparison of Field-Level Risk Definitions}\label{sec:res-def}

\paragraph{Mechanism}
Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism} uses synthetic data with a known structure,
$Y=a\,AB+bP+\sigma\varepsilon$, where $A$ and $B$ act only jointly, $C_1$ and $C_2$ are two
noisy copies of $P$, $D$ is a weak copy of $P$ and $N$ is noise; all 63 sets are retrained.
Correlation, single-field retraining and pairwise inference-graph propagation give $A$ and $B$
a score of zero although $\{A,B\}$ reaches $0.54$. LOCO gives $C_1$ and $C_2$ almost zero because
each substitutes for the other, and LOCO, permutation importance and SAGE all give $D$ zero
because the full model relies on $C_1,C_2$ instead---yet $\{A,B,D\}$ crosses $\tau=0.7$ while
$\{A,B\}$ does not, so $D$ is critical. SAGE splits credit within both pairs. $M^{(2)}$ assigns
$A$, $B$, $C_1$, $C_2$ their full worst-case contribution and gives $D$ a non-zero risk with the
witness $\{A,B\}$. This is the behaviour predicted by Table~\ref{tab:req}: averages dilute,
single contexts miss substitutes, and pairwise edges miss joint inference.

\paragraph{Decisions}
The deliverable of grading is a decision, so we first compare flagging rules
(Table~\ref{tab:decision}, Fig.~\ref{fig:definition}a). The single-field rule recovers 13\% of
the critical fields at $\tau=0.5$ and 17\% at $\tau=0.7$. The scan on amortized estimates
recovers 93\% and 83\%; with certification, which only accepts a field after dedicated
attackers retrained on one of its three best-scanned backgrounds confirm the threshold
crossing, precision is exactly one by construction and recall is 88\% ($\tau=0.5$, $\delta=0$)
and 80\% ($\tau=0.7$, $\delta=0.05$). The margin $\delta$ compensates for the common-mode
underestimation of the estimator (Section~\ref{sec:res-est}), which matters near high
thresholds.

For disposal, the relevant question is how many fields must be withheld to break every
dangerous combination. Withholding what single-field grading flags leaves 98\% ($\tau=0.5$) and
96\% ($\tau=0.7$) of the minimal unsafe sets intact. Two strategies that select fields from
amortized estimates only---a minimum hitting set of the estimated minimal unsafe sets, and a
greedy rule that withholds the field with the largest $\hat M^{(2)}$ and recomputes $\hat
M^{(2)}$ on the remaining fields---leave 1.3--5.8\% intact while withholding at most 1.2 fields
more than the optimum computed from the ground truth (Fig.~\ref{fig:definition}c). The greedy
rule exploits the relative nature of $\Mik$: once a field is withheld, the risk of the others
changes, and $\Mik$ is recomputed with respect to what remains.

\paragraph{Ranking}
Table~\ref{tab:ranking} compares the scores as rankings. At $\tau=0.7$, $\hat M^{(2)}$ has the
highest R-precision (0.903, against 0.866 for mutual information, 0.830 for the inference graph,
0.803 for single-field retraining, 0.769 for SAGE and 0.727 for LOCO), and withholding fields in
the order of exact or certified $M^{(2)}$ exceeds the optimum by only 0.4 fields. At $\tau=0.5$
almost every field is critical and every score reaches an R-precision of about 0.98; here
single-field retraining ranks as well as $M^{(2)}$. We report this plainly: as a \emph{ranking},
single-field risk is a strong baseline when most fields are dangerous; the advantage of $\Mik$
lies in decisions, disposal and high thresholds. Attribution scores computed on one fitted
model (LOCO, permutation importance, SAGE) are the weakest throughout, because they measure what
that model uses rather than what an adversary can infer.
